Gravitational waves

Silicon optics for the Einstein Telescope

The cold silicon optics of the Einstein Telescope must not change the polarisation of light: we map how much they do.

The physics.

The Einstein Tele­scope will detect grav­i­ta­tion­al waves down to a few hertz. Its low-fre­quen­cy in­ter­fer­om­e­ters are planned to run at cryo­gen­ic tem­per­a­ture, with optics of crys­tal­line silicon instead of fused silica, read with in­fra­red light at which silicon is trans­par­ent.

A perfect cubic crystal is opti­cally iso­trop­ic, but stress, defects and im­pu­ri­ties make silicon slight­ly bi­re­frin­gent: the two polarisa­tions of light travel at slight­ly dif­fer­ent speeds. In the arm cav­i­ties and the beam split­ter this turns part of the light into the wrong polarisa­tion, which is lost from the in­ter­fer­om­e­ter.

We built a po­lar­im­e­ter that maps bi­re­frin­gence across a sub­strate. A po­lar­ised laser beam crosses the sample, and the change in its polarisa­tion, mea­sured point by point, gives the size of the bi­re­frin­gence and the direc­tion of its axes.

What we have shown.

The low-fre­quen­cy in­ter­fer­om­e­ters of the Einstein Tele­scope will use cryo­gen­ic silicon optics, where bi­re­frin­gence turns into optical loss. We map bi­re­frin­gence in silicon samples and explain it through the crystal's an­isot­ro­py, and are extend­ing the method to quartz and sap­phire.
