Crystal bonding

Glue-free assemblies that keep their curvature

Bonded to a substrate figured to the right shape, a crystal takes that shape and keeps it, with no glue and no holder.

The physics.

Bent crys­tals are usually curved by me­chan­i­cal holders that press or clamp them. The result is never exactly the in­tend­ed shape: bending a plate one way also curves it the other way (an­ti­clas­tic cur­va­ture), clamps can twist it, and the holder adds mass and bulk, a burden for the go­ni­om­e­ters that align crys­tals to a beam. Glue avoids the holder but leaves a layer that creeps and changes with tem­per­a­ture, so the shape drifts.

Bonding joins the crystal di­rect­ly to a sub­strate figured to the wanted shape with optical preci­sion, and the crystal takes that shape. In anodic bonding silicon and an alkali-contain­ing glass are heated to a few hundred degrees and a voltage of a few hundred volts is applied, with the crystal pos­i­tive. Sodium ions in the glass drift away from the inter­face, the electro­static attrac­tion pulls the sur­faces into full contact, and oxygen from the glass bonds to the silicon.

In thermo-compres­sion bonding two metal layers are pressed to­geth­er while hot until they inter­diffuse; a gold–ger­ma­nium eu­tec­tic joins ger­ma­nium crys­tals. For quartz or lithium niobate we look for sub­strates whose thermal expan­sion matches the crystal, since any mis­match leaves stress and extra cur­va­ture when the as­sem­bly cools.

The same plat­form serves three fam­i­lies of devices: silicon crys­tals for TeV beams at the LHC, bonded to sub­strates with radii of about 10 m for deflec­tions of several mil­li­ra­di­ans; spher­i­cal and cy­lin­dri­cal crys­tals for X-ray spec­tros­co­py and imaging; and rows of aligned tiles for Laue lenses.

What we have shown.

A silicon crystal anodi­cally bonded on a cy­lin­dri­cal lens de­flect­ed high-energy hadrons as well as a con­ven­tion­al bender, with almost no torsion. CRYSTAL BOND, the INFN na­tion­al project we lead with Legnaro, Trieste and Frascati, extends anodic and thermo-compres­sion bonding on pre-shaped sub­strates to new LHC de­flec­tors and to X-ray lenses, from syn­chro­tron en­er­gies to the hard X-rays of Laue lenses. With INAF Brera we adapted sil­i­cate bonding to curved X-ray tele­scope optics, dis­pensed by a col­lab­o­ra­tive robot.
