X-ray tomoscopy

Crystal beam splitters for MHz imaging

Thin crystals split one X-ray beam into several, so a sample is seen from many angles at once, without turning it.

The physics.

X-ray tomography reconstructs the inside of an opaque object from projections taken at many angles, usually by rotating the sample in the beam. Rotation limits the speed, cannot follow events that happen only once, and its centrifugal forces disturb fragile or liquid samples.

X-ray multi-projection imaging (XMPI) takes the projections at the same time. A thin crystal in Laue geometry, set at the Bragg angle, diffracts part of the beam by 2θB and lets the rest through; a few such splitters divide one beam into several, which reach the sample from different directions and fall on separate fast detectors.

Each X-ray pulse then gives a set of views, and successive pulses give a three-dimensional movie. At European XFEL, whose pulses come at megahertz rates, this opens microsecond time resolution; at synchrotrons it already records thousands of frames per second.

The splitters must divide the beam evenly and without distorting it, so the lattice has to be perfect across the illuminated area. We make them from silicon and diamond crystals prepared in Ferrara.

What we have shown.

Instead of rotating the sample, thin crystal beam splitters divide the X-ray beam into several views taken at once. MHz-TOMOSCOPY gave the first experimental demonstration of megahertz multi-projection X-ray imaging, with silicon and diamond crystals made in Ferrara and used at European XFEL, ESRF and MAX IV. The European Commission's Innovation Radar named UniFe and INFN Key Innovators for these silicon optics.

MHz

First experimental demonstration of megahertz X-ray multi-projection imaging, within MHz-TOMOSCOPY, with silicon and diamond splitters made in Ferrara and used at European XFEL, ESRF and MAX IV.

1000 fps

3D dynamics of molten aluminium recorded at the ESRF ID19 beamline, at 1000 frames per second and 8 µm resolution per projection.

Stereo

Hard X-ray stereographic microscopy: two phase-contrast views recorded in a single exposure and combined into a 3D model.
