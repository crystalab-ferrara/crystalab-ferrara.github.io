Crystal bonding

Glue-free assemblies that keep their curvature

Bonded to a substrate figured to the right shape, a crystal takes that shape and keeps it, with no glue and no holder.

The physics.

Bent crystals are usually curved by mechanical holders that press or clamp them. The result is never exactly the intended shape: bending a plate one way also curves it the other way (anticlastic curvature), clamps can twist it, and the holder adds mass and bulk, a burden for the goniometers that align crystals to a beam. Glue avoids the holder but leaves a layer that creeps and changes with temperature, so the shape drifts.

Bonding joins the crystal directly to a substrate figured to the wanted shape with optical precision, and the crystal takes that shape. In anodic bonding silicon and an alkali-containing glass are heated to a few hundred degrees and a voltage of a few hundred volts is applied, with the crystal positive. Sodium ions in the glass drift away from the interface, the electrostatic attraction pulls the surfaces into full contact, and oxygen from the glass bonds to the silicon.

In thermo-compression bonding two metal layers are pressed together while hot until they interdiffuse; a gold–germanium eutectic joins germanium crystals. For quartz or lithium niobate we look for substrates whose thermal expansion matches the crystal, since any mismatch leaves stress and extra curvature when the assembly cools.

The same platform serves three families of devices: silicon crystals for TeV beams at the LHC, bonded to substrates with radii of about 10 m for deflections of several milliradians; spherical and cylindrical crystals for X-ray spectroscopy and imaging; and rows of aligned tiles for Laue lenses. With INAF Brera we adapted silicate bonding, dispensed by a collaborative robot, to the curved optics of X-ray telescopes.

What we have shown.

A silicon crystal anodically bonded on a cylindrical lens deflected high-energy hadrons as well as a conventional bender, with almost no torsion. CRYSTAL BOND, the INFN national project we lead with Legnaro, Trieste and Frascati, extends anodic and thermo-compression bonding on pre-shaped substrates to new LHC deflectors and to X-ray lenses, from synchrotron energies to the hard X-rays of Laue lenses. With INAF Brera we adapted silicate bonding to curved X-ray telescope optics, dispensed by a collaborative robot.
